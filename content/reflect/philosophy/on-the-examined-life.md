In Plato's *Apology*, Socrates offers his most famous line as part of his defence against charges of impiety and corrupting the youth of Athens. He has been condemned to death. He could have escaped. He chose not to. And in explaining why, he said:

> The unexamined life is not worth living for a human being.

It is one of those sentences that seems clear on first reading and becomes stranger the longer you look at it.

## What Examination Means

Socrates was not advocating for introspection in the modern therapeutic sense — the careful tracking of one's feelings, the construction of a coherent personal narrative. His examinations were dialectical. They proceeded through conversation, through question and answer, through the patient dismantling of assumptions.

The goal was not self-knowledge as psychological clarity. It was *epistemic* — knowledge of what one actually knows and, more importantly, what one does not.

The famous result of Socratic examination was typically negative: the interlocutor, who had believed himself to understand justice or courage or piety, discovered that he did not. The examined life begins with this discovery.

## The Demand This Makes

If we take Socrates seriously, the examined life demands something uncomfortable: **willingness to be wrong**. Not merely about trivial matters, but about the things we are most confident in — our values, our identities, our most settled beliefs.

This is harder than it sounds. Our beliefs are not merely intellectual positions. They are load-bearing structures in the architecture of the self. To examine them seriously is to risk discovering that something you have built your life around does not survive scrutiny.

Most people avoid this. The examined life, in Socrates' sense, is genuinely difficult and genuinely rare.

## Is It Worth It?

Socrates thought the alternative — living unreflectively — was a kind of sleep. You might be comfortable. You might be successful. But you would be navigating by a map you had never checked against the territory.

The examined life is not necessarily a happier life. Socrates died for it. But it is a more *honest* one — and, if honesty matters, a more fully human one.

The question he leaves us with is not whether to examine, but whether we have the courage to follow the examination wherever it leads.
